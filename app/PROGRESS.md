## 2026-10-09 · over-correction

- 19:30 Rule: a word that stands on its own (kept, or one that you typing it with every tap on its key's center would get read back as itself, by your touches and the lexicon without your word counts) is replaced only when your touches fit the other word better. Frequencies can choose among readings of your fingers but never overrule them. A missed key or apostrophe never counts against a real word, so "tie" stays "tie" however often you type "time", and "presidents" stays plural. A rare word your own perfect taps would still be read past gets no cover. No constant: both tests compare the model's own likelihoods.
- 19:30 Tried and dropped: requiring the replacement to also win without your word counts (robust Bayes). It kept 98.1–98.3% but broke 6–7 words at σ 0.25. A center prior worth 4 taps (κ₀ = 4) changed nothing, so κ₀ stays 1.
- 19:30 Lexicon now includes SCOWL's British and Canadian spellings (137,578 words, 2.42 MB), so "favourite" and "labour" are words, not typos.

- Now the keystroke only picks the key and inserts it. `TypingEngine.defersSuggestions` leaves the suggestions stale; the controller packs a `SuggestionJob` (the engine with a snapshot of the corrector) and ranks it on a background task, which also ranks the join check the next space will make. Its rankings move into the corrector's cache if the words, touches and grid didn't change since (`Corrector.generation`), so autocorrect on space finds them ready; if the space comes first it ranks that one word itself. Learning on word commit costs ~10 µs and stays on the main thread.

- Design. `TextState` (KeyboardCore) is the one copy of the text the engine reads: the text before and after the caret as the host last showed it, plus every edit the keyboard made since. One write path, `TypingEngine.write(_:replacing:in:)`, deletes and inserts in the host and in `TextState` together, so the two can't drift by the keyboard's own hand. The host's reports come in only through `documentChanged` and are compared, not copied: a report that shows a window of what the keyboard knows is current; one that shows an earlier state (kept as the last 64 characters of each state since the host last caught up, matched to the earliest it could be, since typing and deleting can repeat a state) is late and changes nothing; anything else (caret moved, the app changed or cleared the text, a paste) replaces what the engine knows and forgets the word in progress. A ranking job copies the engine with its `TextState` and has no document to write to; its result applies only at the revision it was ranked for, and a suggestion tap applies only to the word the bar was ranked for. Caret moves from the space bar go through the engine (`moveCursor`), which moves the known text with it.

## 2026-10-12 · aim that follows you

- Bassim: the model feels static while typing; he wants "at least a couple levels of dynamic weighting" with real-time response, so that making the same mistake two or three times in a row changes it on the fly, built minimally after looking at how others do it. Cause, verified in code: every tap counted forever, so after his 11,576 taps a new one moved a key's center by about 1/(taps on that key), and a key needed hundreds of new taps to follow a new grip.
- How others do it. Gboard ([Sivek & Riley 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11311)) keeps the last 800 taps over all keys in 4 buckets, found weighting older buckets down no better than uniform, and wrote that small histories able to follow a posture change are too small to be useful. Google's hierarchical spatial backoff model ([Yin et al., CHI 2013](https://research.google/pubs/making-touchscreen-keyboards-adaptive-to-keys-hand-postures-and-individuals-a-hierarchical-spatial-backoff-model-approach/)) and ContextType ([Goel et al., CHI 2013](https://ubicomplab.cs.washington.edu/publications/contexttype)) keep a model per hand posture and switch on a posture classifier (13.2% and 20.6% fewer errors in lab studies). Personal offset models need a few hundred taps ([Weir et al., UIST 2012](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/68064)) and carry across devices once adapted ([Buschek et al., MobileHCI 2013](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/80621)). Tracking whichever predictor is best right now is fixed share ([Herbster & Warmuth 1998](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1007424614876)).
- Design: each key's center is remembered three ways, over all its taps, over about its last 30 and over about its last 3 (older taps fade by 1 − 1/n as each new tap of that key arrives, never on a clock). Spread stays learned from all taps, so a miss moves where a key aims, not how sloppy it thinks you are. Each memory's weight is the posterior that it predicts the key's next tap best, updated by Bayes on every learned tap, with a 1-in-100 share kept for each so any can take over (fixed share). The three are blended into one Student-t by their weighted mean and spread, so the key map and the decoder cost the same as before. The decoder's readings of a word count taps toward steadiness only (`Layout.suppose`), leaving recent aim alone.
- Per-posture models (one cloud per hand) not built: a posture switch is a shift the short memories already catch in two or three taps per key, and the all-taps memory takes the key back once the old grip returns. They'd pay off only for someone who switches hands constantly; Gboard didn't predict posture either.
- Saved models load: a key saved as plain stats starts both short memories where all its taps center (his phone's model from `build/phone/touches.json` decodes, centers within 0.01 key, round-trips equal). `MemoryCodingTests.keysSavedBeforeTheyRememberedRecentAimStillLoad`.
